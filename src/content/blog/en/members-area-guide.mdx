Astro Rocket can now put a lock on your content. A reader signs in with their
email address — no password to choose, nothing to reset — and reads what their
membership covers.

There is no database anywhere in it, and no membership platform taking a cut.

## One line

Put this in a post's frontmatter:

```yaml
access: members
```

That post now needs a signed-in member. Nothing else about it changes: same
URL, same layout, still listed on the blog.

If you run more than one level, name a tier instead:

```yaml
access: pro
```

Omit the field and the post is public, which is every post until you decide
otherwise.

## What a visitor sees

A gated post keeps its place in the listing as a locked card carrying the
title, the date and the description. That matters: nobody signs up for
something they cannot see the shape of.

Open it without signing in and you get those same words, a lock, and a way to
sign in. **The body is not in that response at all** — it is not hidden with
CSS or blurred behind an overlay, it is never sent.

Gated posts are also kept out of the RSS feed and the search index. Feeds get
republished, and a members-only post has no business travelling that way.

## Turning it on

Two environment variables:

```bash
MEMBERS_SESSION_SECRET=   # openssl rand -base64 32
RESEND_API_KEY=           # sends the links
RESEND_FROM_EMAIL=        # the address they come from
```

Then `src/config/members.config.ts`:

```ts
enabled: true,
tiers: ['pro'],
members: [
  { email: 'anna@example.com', name: 'Anna' },
  { email: 'ben@example.com', name: 'Ben', tiers: ['pro'] },
],
```

Adding a member is a line and a deploy. The list lives in your repository,
which means the record of who had access, and when, is your git history.

**You can try it before signing up for anything.** In development the sign-in
link prints to your terminal instead of being emailed. Run `pnpm dev`, enter
an address, click the link in your console.

## What it is for

The same build serves all of these, because they are all content rather than
dashboards:

- A **paid newsletter** — the archive public, new posts for members.
- A **course** — lessons in order, later modules on a higher tier.
- A **club or association** — minutes, schedules and forms for members.
- **Trade pricing** — price lists shown to trade customers, hidden from the
  public.
- A **supporter area** — extra material for people who chip in.
- **Client resources** — guides and files for clients on a care plan.

None of them needs a line of custom code.

## Off by default, and off means absent

The theme ships with this switched off. Not hidden — absent. No member routes
are built, and a site that never turns it on builds exactly as it did before
the feature existed, with no serverless function where it had none.

Two rules follow from that, and both are deliberate:

A post marked `access: members` while the feature is off **disappears** rather
than becoming public. A gate that is switched off must not publish what it was
hiding.

Enabling it without a session secret **stops the build**. A gate that fails
open is worse than no gate, and it would look like it was working the whole
time.

## The honest limits

Four things this does not do, which are worth knowing before you build on it.

**A session cannot be revoked before it expires.** Removing someone from the
list stops them signing in again, but a cookie already issued lives out its 30
days. There is no session store to invalidate — that is the trade for needing
no database. Shorten `sessionDays` if that matters to you.

**A sign-in link is valid until it expires, not until it is used once.**
Recording that a token had been used would need somewhere to record it. Links
last 15 minutes for that reason.

**The sign-in brake is per server instance.** Five attempts a minute per
address, held in memory. It stops the obvious loop; put your host's firewall
in front if you need a real limiter.

**No self-signup, no payments, and no per-customer records.** Members are a
list you keep.

## What it deliberately does not do

That last limit is the one worth dwelling on, because it is a decision rather
than an omission.

Everything above works because it gates content the theme already renders —
your posts, your pages. That is why it needs no dashboard: what a member lands
on is a filtered list of your own content, which is the same thing your blog
index already is.

A dashboard showing someone *their own records* — their invoices, their
project, their progress through a course — is a different thing. It cannot be
generic, because the records are different for every business. A theme that
shipped one would be shipping a screen you would have to delete.

So this ships the door, and the room behind it stays yours.
