Add one line to a post and it stops being public:

```yaml
access: members
```

That is the whole change. The post keeps its URL, keeps its place on the blog,
and keeps its title, date and description on the card. The words themselves now
need a signed-in member.

No database. No membership platform taking a percentage. No password for anyone
to choose, forget, or have leaked.

## Try it on this page, in ten seconds

This site is running it, so you do not have to take my word for any of the
above. There is no signup and no email address involved:

1. Open [A Members-Only Post](/blog/members-only-example). You will see the
   title, the date and the description, and a lock where the article should be.
2. Click **Sign in**.
3. On the sign-in page, click **Sign in as a demo member**.
4. Go back to the post. It is all there.

Then sign out from `/members` and it locks again.

Four clicks, nothing to undo, nothing given away. If you only do one thing with
this post, do that — the rest is explanation.

## There is no signup. You decide who gets in.

Worth being exact about this, because a form asking for an email address
usually means self-service, and this one does not.

Nobody can let themselves in. A person becomes a member when **you** put their
address on the list — after they bought the course, signed the contract, joined
the club, paid the invoice, whatever the arrangement is. That part happens
away from the site: an email to you, a checkout somewhere else, a conversation.
Then you add a line and deploy.

Until their address is on that list, the form does nothing for them. They can
type it and press the button, they will see the same *check your email* page
everyone sees, and no link will ever arrive.

**The sameness is on purpose.** If the form answered "no such member", it would
become a way to find out who your members are: type addresses until one is
recognised. A list of your clients is worth more to a stranger than it sounds.
So the page answers identically either way, and only real members get an email.

**One consequence is yours to handle.** Someone who ought to be a member but is
not on the list yet will wait for a link that is never coming. Give them a way
to ask — a line under the sign-in form, or a sentence in the locked post's
description saying how to get access. The theme cannot know what your
arrangement is; it only enforces the list.

## What happens to a gated post

Five things, and the third is the one that matters:

**It stays on the blog** as a locked card carrying the title, the date and the
description. Nobody signs up for something they cannot see the shape of, so the
value stays visible.

**Opening it without signing in** gives those same words, a lock, and a way in.
Not a 404, and not a page that looks broken.

**The body is not in that response.** It is not hidden with CSS, not blurred
behind an overlay, not sitting in the page waiting for JavaScript to reveal it.
It is never sent. View source on a locked post and the article is not there.

**It is kept out of the RSS feed and the search index.** Feeds get republished,
and a members-only post has no business travelling that way.

**It renders on demand while the rest of your site stays static.** Only the
gated posts stop being prerendered; the other ninety pages build exactly as
they did.

## How it works underneath

Worth knowing, because it explains both what it does well and what it will not
do.

A sign-in link is a short-lived signed token — your address, your tiers, and an
expiry, signed with a secret only your server knows. Clicking it swaps that
token for a session cookie, signed the same way.

**There is no session table anywhere.** The cookie *is* the session. That is
what lets the whole feature run with no database, on the same static hosting
you already use, with nothing else to pay for or keep alive.

Tiers ride along in the same cookie. `access: members` needs any member;
`access: pro` needs someone holding that tier.

## Turning it on for your own site

Three steps.

**One — two environment variables:**

```bash
MEMBERS_SESSION_SECRET=   # openssl rand -base64 32
RESEND_API_KEY=           # sends the sign-in links
RESEND_FROM_EMAIL=        # the address they come from
```

**Two — enable it and list your members** in `src/config/members.config.ts`:

```ts
enabled: true,
tiers: ['pro'],
members: [
  { email: 'anna@example.com', name: 'Anna' },
  { email: 'ben@example.com', name: 'Ben', tiers: ['pro'] },
],
```

**Three — gate something.** `access: members` in any post's frontmatter.

Adding a member from then on is one line and a deploy. The list lives in your
repository, which means the record of who had access, and when, is your git
history — no admin screen to build, and none to secure.

### You can try it locally without signing up for anything

Before you go near Resend: in development the sign-in link is **printed to your
terminal** instead of being emailed.

```bash
pnpm dev
```

Open `/members/login`, type any address on your list, and the link appears in
your console. Click it and you are signed in. The entire feature works on your
own machine with no account anywhere.

## What people build with it

All of these are the same build, because all of them are content rather than
dashboards:

- **A paid newsletter** — the archive public, new posts for members.
- **A course** — lessons in order, later modules on a higher tier.
- **A club or association** — minutes, schedules and forms behind a sign-in.
- **Trade pricing** — price lists shown to trade customers, hidden from the
  public.
- **A supporter area** — extra material for people who chip in.
- **Client resources** — guides, files and instructions for clients on a care
  plan.

None of them needs a line of custom code. You mark content and the theme does
the rest.

## Nothing changes until you turn it on

If you are upgrading an existing site and want none of this, you already have
what you want. The members area ships disabled, and disabled means **absent**,
not hidden: no member routes are built, and your site produces the same output
it did before this feature existed — including no serverless function where you
had none.

Two rules follow, and both are deliberate:

A post marked `access: members` while the feature is off **disappears** rather
than becoming public. A gate that is switched off must not publish what it was
hiding.

Enabling it without a session secret **stops the build** with a message telling
you how to make one. A gate that fails open is worse than no gate, because it
would look like it was working the whole time.

## The limits, stated plainly

Four, and you should know them before you build on it rather than after.

**A session cannot be revoked early.** Removing someone from the list stops
them signing in again, but a cookie already issued lives out its thirty days.
There is no session store to invalidate — that is the trade for needing no
database. Shorten `sessionDays` if it matters.

**A link is valid until it expires, not until it is used once.** Recording that
a token had been used would need somewhere to record it. Links last fifteen
minutes for that reason.

**The sign-in brake is per server instance.** Five attempts a minute per
address, held in memory. It stops the obvious loop; put your host's firewall in
front for anything stronger.

**No self-signup, no payments, no per-customer records.** Members are a list
you keep.

## Where it stops, and what to do about it

That last limit is a decision rather than an omission, and it is worth being
clear about.

Everything above works because it gates content the theme already renders —
your posts, your pages. That is why there is no dashboard to design: what a
member lands on is a filtered list of your own content, which is the same thing
your blog index already is.

A screen showing someone *their own records* — their invoices, their project,
their progress through a course — is a different problem. The records are
different for every business, so no theme can ship one that fits. A theme that
tried would be shipping a screen you had to delete.
